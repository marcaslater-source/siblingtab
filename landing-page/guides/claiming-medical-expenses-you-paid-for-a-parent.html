Claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent

If you pay for prescriptions, dental work, hearing aids or care for a parent who depends on you, you may be able to claim those costs on your own return. Here's what counts, how the threshold works, and the receipts CRA expects.

Can you claim your parent's expenses?

Medical expenses for a parent or grandparent go on line 33199 of your return. CRA allows the claim when:

  • the person is your (or your spouse's or common-law partner's) parent, grandparent, brother, sister, aunt, uncle, niece or nephew, or an adult child or grandchild,
  • they lived in Canada at some point in the year (for relatives other than children and grandchildren), and
  • they depended on you for support.

Unlike the caregiver credit, this claim doesn't require your parent to have an infirmity. It's about whether they relied on you for support and whether you paid eligible expenses for them. You can only claim the part that wasn't (and won't be) reimbursed, for example by a health plan.

How the threshold works

Only expenses above a threshold count. For line 33199, it's calculated using your parent's income, not yours, and separately for each dependant. You subtract the lesser of:

  • 3% of your parent's net income (line 23600 of their return), or
  • $2,834 (the 2025 figure; it rises with inflation each year).

Example

Your parent's net income is $30,000. 3% of that is $900, which is less than $2,834, so the threshold is $900.

You paid $5,000 in eligible expenses for them. You can claim $5,000 − $900 = $4,100. At the 14% lowest federal rate (2026), that's about $574 off your federal tax, plus a provincial credit.

Because the threshold uses your parent's income, a parent with a modest pension often has a low threshold, which makes the claim more valuable than many families expect.

Pick the best 12 months

You don't have to use the calendar year. CRA lets you claim expenses paid in any 12-month period ending in the tax year, as long as you didn't claim them the year before. For the 2025 return, July 1, 2024 to June 30, 2025 is a valid choice. If a big expense landed in the fall, picking a window that captures it can make a difference. (If your parent died during the year, a 24-month window that includes the date of death may apply.)

What counts

CRA's guide RC4065, Medical Expenses has the full list. Common ones for aging parents include:

  • Prescription drugs and pharmacy-dispensed medications
  • Dental services, dentures and dental implants
  • Prescription glasses and contact lenses
  • Hearing aids, including repairs and batteries
  • Wheelchairs and wheelchair carriers
  • Catheters, diapers and disposable briefs needed because of illness, injury or a medical condition
  • Premiums for private health plans, if 90% or more of the premiums are for eligible medical expenses

Some items need a prescription or written certification from a medical practitioner to count. Check the specific item in RC4065 before you claim it.

Home care, retirement homes and nursing homes

Care costs are often the largest expense, and the rules are the most detailed. In broad strokes, from CRA's page on attendant care and care in a facility and guide RC4065:

  • Attendant care salaries and wages generally require your parent to have an approved disability tax credit certificate (Form T2201).
  • If the disability amount is also being claimed for your parent, attendant care is generally capped at $10,000 a year ($20,000 in the year of death).
  • Full-time care in a nursing home: you can generally claim either the fees as medical expenses or the disability amount, not both.
  • Retirement homes: the full monthly fee generally isn't claimable, but the care portion (salaries and wages for attendant care) may be if your parent qualifies for the disability tax credit. Ask the residence for an itemized receipt that separates care from room and board.

Because the right choice depends on your parent's situation and which credits they already use, this is a good place to get a tax professional's help.

Travel to appointments

If the treatment your parent needs isn't available closer to home:

  • 40 km or more one way: public transportation, or vehicle expenses if transit isn't readily available, can be claimed.
  • 80 km or more one way: accommodation, meals and parking can be claimed as well.

Trips under 40 km don't count, and neither do trips only to pick up medication or a device. Log the date, destination and kilometres for each trip.

Receipts and records

You don't send receipts with your return, but CRA can ask for them. Keep:

  • Receipts showing who was paid, what for, the date, and the patient's name
  • The prescribing practitioner, where a prescription is required
  • The caregiver's social insurance number on receipts for attendant care or therapy paid to an individual
  • Proof that you paid, such as bank or credit card statements
  • Proof that your parent depended on you for support

The hardest part for most families isn't the rules. It's that the receipts are spread across several siblings' wallets, inboxes and kitchen drawers. Collect them as you go.

Who in the family should claim

  • The same expense can't be claimed twice. Agree which sibling claims each receipt, usually whoever paid.
  • Your parent may be able to claim it themselves on line 33099, if they paid and have tax to reduce. Compare before deciding.
  • Non-refundable means it helps someone who owes tax. A claim does nothing for a sibling with no tax payable.
  • CRA's pages don't spell out how several family members can share one dependant's expenses. If you want to split costs between siblings, ask CRA or a tax professional how to do it properly.
