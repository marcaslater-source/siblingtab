Disability tax credit for a parent: how to apply and transfer it

The disability tax credit (DTC) can lower a parent's income tax. If your parent doesn't owe enough tax to use it, an adult child who supports them may be able to claim it instead. It also opens the door to other claims. Here's how applying and transferring works.

What the credit is

The disability tax credit is a non-refundable credit for people with a severe and prolonged impairment, or for a supporting family member. It's meant to offset some of the extra costs that come with the impairment. Approval by the CRA is what counts: once your parent is approved, they claim it on line 31600 of their own return.

Who is eligible

The CRA decides based on a medical practitioner's certification that the person has a severe and prolonged impairment. Having a diagnosis isn't enough on its own; what matters is how the impairment affects daily life. The CRA's eligibility page sets out the criteria. The people who help your parent day to day, and their doctor, are best placed to say whether it's worth applying.

How to apply

You apply with Form T2201, Disability Tax Credit Certificate. It has two parts:

  • Part A is completed by your parent or their legal representative.
  • Part B is completed only by a medical practitioner. If the applicant fills in Part B, the application won't be processed.

Pick one method for both parts, digital or paper. The CRA can't process a mix of the two. For the digital route, Part A is done through your parent's CRA account (or by phone) and gives a reference number valid for up to 12 months and usable once, which the practitioner enters in the CRA's digital application. For paper, Part A and Part B go together to a tax centre.

Submit the application before you file the tax return; filing the two together may delay the assessment. Practitioners may charge a fee for completing the form. The applicant pays it, but it may be claimable as a medical expense (see claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent). The CRA's how to apply page has the current steps and the number to call.

Transferring it to a supporting family member

Any unused amount isn't refunded; it can be transferred to a supporting family member. The CRA says the supporter must be someone the person with the impairment depends on for at least one of the basic necessities of life: food, shelter or clothing. Children, grandchildren, siblings and other relatives can qualify. The supporter claims it on line 31800.

  • If a supporter wasn't named on the original application, they can send the CRA a signed written request describing the support provided.
  • If two or more people support the same person, they can split the claim, but together they can't claim more than the maximum for that person.

The CRA's claiming the credit page explains it. This is the same "depends on you" idea as in how to claim the Canada caregiver credit, so read the two together before you decide who claims what.

Claiming past years

If your parent was eligible in earlier years but didn't claim, you can generally go back up to 10 years. If the box in Part A of the application is checked, the CRA automatically reassesses past returns; otherwise returns can be changed through "Change my return" in a CRA account, or by mailing a letter asking the CRA to adjust them.

Recent amounts

For adults (age 18 or older), the CRA lists the disability amount as follows. The credit is the amount multiplied by the lowest federal rate, plus any provincial credit, so the tax saved is smaller than the amount shown.

Practical tips for siblings

  • Agree who is the supporter before the return is filed, so the same amount isn't claimed twice. Our guide to splitting a parent's care costs covers how to settle this.
  • Keep the approval letter and any written request you sent to the CRA, in the shared folder.
  • Check what else it unlocks. Home accessibility claims can be made for a person who is eligible for the DTC (see home accessibility and renovation credits).
  • Ask a tax professional if your parent has little income. That's often when a transfer is worth the most.
