Home accessibility and renovation tax credits for a parent's home

Two federal credits can help when a parent's home needs changes: one for accessibility renovations and one for adding a separate suite so a parent can live with family. Here's what each covers, and what to check before you spend.

Home Accessibility Tax Credit

The Home Accessibility Tax Credit (line 31285) is a non-refundable credit for permanent renovations that make a home more accessible or reduce the risk of injury. The CRA's key points:

  • Limit: up to $20,000 of eligible expenses per year for 2022 to 2025 (it was $10,000 for 2015 to 2021). The cap applies per qualifying person and per dwelling.
  • Qualifying individual: someone who is eligible for the disability tax credit at any time in the year, or who is 65 or older at the end of the year. A parent aged 65 or over qualifies on age alone.
  • Who can claim: the qualifying person, or an eligible individual such as a spouse or certain relatives, including children, who meet the CRA's conditions.
  • Eligible home: generally a home in Canada that the qualifying person owns and ordinarily lives in. A relative's home can also qualify in some situations, for example when both live there and the qualifying person owns and lives in no other Canadian home.
  • Eligible costs: permanent renovations, including building materials, fixtures, equipment rentals, plans, permits and professional labour. Your own labour and tools don't count.
  • Not eligible: routine maintenance, appliances, entertainment devices, housekeeping, security monitoring, gardening, financing costs, and renovations mainly meant to raise a home's value.
  • Related contractors: work done by a relative is generally not eligible unless that person is registered for GST/HST.
  • Overlap with medical expenses: eligible medical expenses can be claimed as medical expenses and as home accessibility expenses. Government grants and loans generally don't reduce the eligible amount.

Keep invoices, receipts and proof of payment. Don't send them with the return, but have them ready if the CRA asks.

Multigenerational Home Renovation Tax Credit

The Multigenerational Home Renovation Tax Credit (line 45355) is for renovations that create a self-contained secondary unit so a senior, or an adult who is eligible for the disability tax credit, can live with a qualifying relative. The CRA says:

  • Refundable: you can get it even if you owe no tax.
  • Amount (2025 tax year): 14.5% of costs, on up to $50,000 of qualifying expenses, for a maximum credit of $7,250. For 2023, the CRA's earlier tax tip described 15% and a $7,500 maximum, so check the rate for the year of your claim.
  • The unit: it must have a private entrance, a kitchen and a bathroom.
  • One per lifetime: you can claim one qualifying renovation during the lifetime of a qualifying individual.
  • Paperwork: keep agreements, invoices and receipts. Invoices must show they were paid in full, or come with other proof of payment such as a credit card slip or cancelled cheque.

The CRA's pages for this credit spell out who counts as a qualifying individual and a qualifying relation, and what expenses are covered. Read them before signing a contract.

Before you start the work

  • Confirm with the CRA or a tax professional which credit fits, and who can claim, particularly when the work is on a parent's home but a child pays.
  • Ask contractors for itemized invoices that separate eligible work from the rest.
  • Check local and provincial rules too; permits are often needed, and some provinces offer their own grants or credits.
  • Pay and document in the same tax year you plan to claim in.

When siblings share the cost

Renovations are often the biggest single expense. Agree in writing who is paying what and who claims, because only one claim can be made for each expense.
