How to claim the Canada caregiver credit for a parent

If your mom or dad depends on you because of a physical or mental condition, you may be able to claim the Canada caregiver credit. Here's who qualifies, what it's worth, and how siblings can share it.

What the caregiver credit is

The Canada caregiver credit is a federal tax credit for people who support a family member with a mental or physical infirmity. It's non-refundable: it lowers the tax you owe, but it won't create a refund on its own if you don't owe any tax. Most provinces have their own caregiver credit on the provincial part of your return as well.

For an adult child supporting a parent, the claim usually goes on line 30450.

Does your parent qualify?

According to CRA, you can claim line 30450 for a dependant who meets all of these conditions:

  • Relationship. They're your parent or grandparent, or your spouse's or common-law partner's parent or grandparent. (Brothers, sisters, aunts, uncles, nieces and nephews can also qualify.)
  • Age. They're 18 or older.
  • Lived in Canada. They lived in Canada at some point in the year.
  • Infirmity. They depend on you because of a mental or physical infirmity that lasts a considerable period. CRA is clear that a temporary illness or injury doesn't count, and that being a parent or grandparent isn't enough on its own.
  • Dependent on you. CRA describes this as regularly and consistently relying on you for basic necessities such as food, shelter and clothing.
  • Income. Their net income (line 23600 of their return) was under $28,798 for 2025. Above $20,197 the credit starts to shrink (more on that below).
  • No overlapping claim. No one has claimed them as a spouse or eligible dependant on line 30300 or 30400.

CRA's line 30450 page doesn't require your parent to live with you, so a parent in their own home or in a care facility can still qualify if the other conditions are met.

How much it's worth

For the 2025 tax year, the caregiver amount on line 30450 is up to $8,601 per eligible dependant. It's reduced dollar for dollar once your parent's net income goes over $20,197, and disappears at $28,798.

That $8,601 is not what you get back. Federal non-refundable credits are worth the amount times the lowest federal tax rate, which is 14% for 2026. So a full claim takes roughly $1,200 off your federal tax, plus whatever your province adds.

All of these amounts rise with inflation each January. Federal amounts were indexed by 2.0% for 2026, so expect a maximum around $8,773 on your 2026 return. Schedule 5 of the return does the reduction math for you.

A change to watch

Budget 2026 proposes making the caregiver credit refundable starting with the 2027 tax year, so caregivers with little or no tax owing could still benefit. This is a proposal and isn't law yet; the rules above apply for 2025 and 2026.

Which line to claim it on

  • Line 30450 is the usual line for a parent or grandparent 18 or older.
  • Line 30425 is used instead if you're claiming your parent as an eligible dependant on line 30400. That generally applies when you're single, separated or widowed and supporting the parent in a home you maintain. If that sounds like you, read CRA's line 30425 page first: the eligible dependant amount plus line 30425 can be worth more than line 30450 alone.

Either way, the calculation is done on Schedule 5. Tax software will ask you about your dependants and fill it in.

When more than one sibling helps

This is where families get tripped up. CRA's rule for line 30450 is simple: if you and someone else both supported the same dependant, you can split the claim, but the total can't be more than the maximum for that dependant.

  • Two siblings can't each claim the full $8,601 for the same parent. If CRA sees two full claims, expect a reassessment.
  • You can split it any way that adds up to the maximum or less: all to one sibling, half and half, or something else.
  • It only helps a sibling who owes federal tax. If one of you has little taxable income, the credit is usually worth more to someone else.

Agree on the split before anyone files, write it down, and give everyone the same numbers. Our guide to splitting a parent's care costs fairly covers how to fold this into the wider conversation about money.

What proof you need

You don't send anything with your return. Keep these in case CRA asks:

  • A signed statement from a medical practitioner saying when the infirmity began and how long it's expected to last. CRA says you may need it. You don't need one if CRA already has an approved disability tax credit certificate (Form T2201) for your parent for that period.
  • Proof that your parent depends on you, such as records of money you spent on their groceries, rent, or care.
  • Your parent's net income for the year (from their notice of assessment).
  • The split you agreed with your siblings, if you're sharing the claim.

Step by step

  1. Confirm your parent has a long-lasting infirmity and relies on you for basic needs. If you're unsure, ask their doctor or nurse practitioner for a signed statement now; it's easier than chasing one if CRA asks.
  2. Find your parent's net income for the year (line 23600 of their return).
  3. Check whether anyone is claiming them on line 30300 or 30400. If you are, use line 30425 instead.
  4. Agree with your siblings who claims how much, keeping the total at or under the maximum.
  5. Enter your share in your tax software (it fills Schedule 5 and line 30450), and claim the provincial caregiver amount too.
  6. Keep the doctor's statement, income figure and your written split with your tax records.

Missed it in past years? You can ask CRA to adjust earlier returns, generally up to 10 years back, through "Change my return" in CRA My Account.

Other credits to check at the same time

  • Medical expenses you paid for your parent go on line 33199. See claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent.
  • The disability tax credit. If your parent is approved for the disability tax credit but doesn't need all of it to bring their own tax to zero, the unused part can transfer to a supporting family member on line 31800.
