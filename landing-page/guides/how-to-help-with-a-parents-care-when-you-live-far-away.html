How to help with a parent's care when you live far away

If you live in another city, it can feel as though there is nothing useful you can do except send money and feel guilty. There is a lot more you can take on. Here are practical ways to carry real weight from a distance, and to make sure the sibling nearby feels it.

Start by asking

Ask the sibling nearby, "What is the one thing that would take the most off your plate this month?" Then listen, and do it reliably. A small task you complete every time is worth more than a large offer that fades after a week. If you can, ask your parent too; they may want something different from what the rest of the family assumes.

Tasks you can own from anywhere

  • Phone and online calls: the doctor's office, the pharmacy, the insurer, the care agency, the bank.
  • Booking and coordinating: appointments, transportation, a PSW, an adult day program, home repairs, meal delivery.
  • Research: comparing care options, retirement homes and costs. See what home care and long-term care cost in Canada.
  • Paperwork: forms, applications and renewals, including possible tax credits such as the disability tax credit.
  • Keeping the shared record: one place where every expense and receipt is logged, so the sibling nearby does not also have to be the bookkeeper.
  • Regular calls with your parent, scheduled, so they have something to look forward to and you hear how they are really doing.

Pick two or three that fit your skills and your schedule, and say plainly that they are yours.

The money side

Money is often the easiest thing for a distant sibling to contribute, and the easiest to get wrong. A few ways to do it well:

  • Agree the amount in advance, rather than sending money in bursts when you feel guilty or the other sibling asks.
  • Cover the nearby sibling's costs, such as gas, parking, and time off work, so those do not come out of one person's pocket.
  • Pay for respite. Paying for a PSW or day program for a few hours a week is a concrete gift of time to whoever is doing the hands-on care.
  • Be transparent. Say what you can afford and why. See how to split a parent's care costs fairly.

Making visits count

  • Plan visits to give the sibling nearby a real break, not just a second person to host. Ask what they will do with the time off.
  • Take over the full routine for a few days: appointments, meals, medication, and the nights. It is the best way to understand what the sibling nearby does week in and week out.
  • Use the time for jobs that need two people, such as sorting paperwork, clearing a room, or a conversation about the future.
  • Count the cost of travel in the family discussion. Some families treat flights home as a personal choice, others as part of the shared cost. Decide it up front. A family meeting is a good place to settle it; see how to hold a family meeting about a parent's care.

Staying informed

  • Agree a regular update, for example a short call every week or two, so the nearby sibling does not have to chase you or repeat themselves.
  • Ask for the actual numbers: what care costs, what was spent, what is coming. Seeing it makes it real.
  • With your parent's permission, ask to be listed as a contact with their doctor, pharmacy or care home, so you hear things directly.

What the sibling nearby needs to hear

Often the most valuable thing a distant sibling can offer is acknowledgement. Say it directly: "I can see how much you are doing. Thank you. What can I take on?" Avoid giving advice on how things should be done unless you are also taking on the work. If you disagree about a decision, raise it as a question, and be ready to help with whichever option the family chooses. If you are on the other side of this, see when one sibling does most of the caregiving.

Tax and paperwork

Where you pay for a parent's care or medical costs, keep the receipts and agree with your siblings who will claim what, so the same expense is not claimed twice. See claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent and how to claim the Canada caregiver credit. Whether you can claim anything depends on your situation, including whether your parent depended on you for support, so check the CRA pages below or ask a tax professional.
