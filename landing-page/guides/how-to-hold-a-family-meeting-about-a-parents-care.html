Family caregiving

How to hold a family meeting about a parent's care

A well-run family meeting can settle in an hour what months of texting cannot. Here is who to invite, a simple agenda you can copy, ground rules that keep it calm, and what to do afterwards so it sticks.

Why a meeting beats a group text

Group chats are good for logistics and poor for hard conversations. Tone gets lost, people reply at different times, and the loudest message wins. A scheduled conversation, in person or by video, gives everyone the same information at the same time and a clear moment to agree on what happens next.

Who should be there

  • Every sibling who will be affected, including those who live far away. Video is fine.
  • Your parent, where they are able and willing. It is their life and their money. If they cannot take part in the whole meeting, ask for their wishes beforehand and share them.
  • Partners only if everyone agrees. They are often affected, but a meeting can quickly become two-against-three.
  • An outside facilitator, if the family has a history of conflict. This might be an aunt or uncle, a family friend, a social worker or care coordinator, or a professional mediator.

Before the meeting

  1. Pick a date and send an agenda a week ahead, so nobody is ambushed. A simple message works: "I would like us to talk about how we share Mom's care. Here is what I would like to cover."
  2. Prepare one page of facts: what care is in place, what it costs each month, who is doing what, and what is coming (a move, a new diagnosis, a change in home care). Send it in advance.
  3. Know your parent's own resources (pension, savings, insurance) so you are not asking siblings to cover what your parent can pay. See what home care and long-term care cost in Canada.
  4. Decide what you are asking for. Go in with one or two specific proposals, not a general grievance.

A simple agenda

Ninety minutes is plenty. You can copy this:

  1. Check-in (5 minutes). How is everyone, and how is Mom or Dad doing?
  2. Where things stand (15 minutes). Walk through the one-page summary: care, costs, who does what. Questions only, no judgement yet.
  3. What is coming (10 minutes). Likely changes over the next six to twelve months.
  4. Your parent's wishes (10 minutes). What do they want, and what would they like each of you to do?
  5. Dividing the work (20 minutes). Tasks first (appointments, paperwork, visits, home), then money. Each person says what they can realistically take on.
  6. Dividing the costs (15 minutes). Equal, by income, or a mix. See how to split a parent's care costs fairly.
  7. Legal and money housekeeping (5 minutes). Is there a power of attorney in place? Who keeps the receipts and the tax paperwork? See power of attorney: who manages a parent's money.
  8. Wrap-up (10 minutes). Read back what was agreed, who owns each task, and the date of the next check-in.

Ground rules

  • One person speaks at a time and others do not interrupt.
  • Speak about your own situation ("I am finding this hard") rather than other people's character ("you never").
  • Past grievances go on a parking list. The meeting is about what happens from here.
  • Everyone gets to say what they can and cannot do, and "I cannot do that, but I can do this" is an acceptable answer.
  • Nothing is decided by silence. If someone has not spoken, ask them.

If it gets heated

Call a ten-minute break rather than pushing through. Come back to the task in front of you. If you cannot agree on something, write down the open question and agree who will find out the missing information, such as a cost, a quote, or what your parent wants. If the same conflict keeps coming back, a mediator can help; many work with families on elder-care disputes, sometimes in a single session. After the meeting

  1. Send a written summary the same day: what was decided, who is doing what, the monthly contribution if any, and the date of the next check-in. Ask everyone to reply "yes" or flag a correction.
  2. Set up one shared record of spending and receipts that everyone can see, so nobody has to rely on memory.
  3. Agree how often you will check in. A short monthly call is enough for many families, and a review whenever something big changes.
  4. Sort out the tax side. Agree who claims which credits so the same receipt is not claimed twice. See the Canada caregiver credit and claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent.
