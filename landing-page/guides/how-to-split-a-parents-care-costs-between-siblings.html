Family money

How to split a parent's care costs fairly between siblings

There's no single right formula. What matters is that everyone agrees on one, it's written down, and the numbers are visible to all of you. Here are the common approaches, how to count time as well as money, and the tax rules that affect who should pay for what.

Last reviewed November 13, 2026

General information, not tax advice. Tax rules change every year and every family is different. We link to the official Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) page for each rule; check it, or talk to a tax professional, before you file.

Start with your parent's own money

Before deciding what siblings owe, work out what your parent can cover from their own income and savings: pensions, CPP and OAS, investments, or the proceeds of selling a home. Many families find the shortfall is smaller (or larger) than they assumed once it's on paper.

  • If a sibling manages your parent's money under a power of attorney, keep your parent's spending in their own account and separate from anyone else's, with receipts. That protects everyone if questions come up later.
  • Don't forget your parent's own tax credits. Medical expenses and the disability tax credit can sometimes be claimed by your parent, or by a supporting family member. Whoever gets more benefit should usually claim.

List what actually needs paying for

Disagreements often start because siblings are picturing different costs. Make one list together. Common items:

  • Care: home care or PSW hours, adult day programs, respite, retirement or long-term care fees
  • Health: prescriptions, dental, glasses, hearing aids, mobility aids, incontinence supplies, insurance premiums
  • Home: rent or property costs, utilities, groceries, repairs, grab bars and ramps
  • Getting around: gas, parking at the hospital, taxis, flights for siblings who live far away
  • Small recurring things that add up: phone plans, meal delivery, pharmacy runs

Then agree which of these count as shared costs. A common sticking point: does a sibling's flight home to visit count? There's no right answer, but it's better decided up front than argued about later.

Four ways to split

1. Equal shares

Everyone pays the same share of shared costs. It's simple and feels fair when siblings have similar incomes and similar involvement. It feels unfair when one sibling earns far less, or does most of the hands-on care.

2. In proportion to income

Each sibling pays a share that matches their share of combined income. For example, if three siblings earn $120,000, $80,000 and $40,000, they'd pay 50%, 33% and 17%. This reflects ability to pay, but requires siblings to share income figures, which some families won't want to do. A rougher version is to agree on percentages without disclosing exact numbers.

3. Money from some, time from others

The sibling who lives nearby handles appointments and visits; siblings further away contribute more money. This is very common, and works best when the trade is stated out loud rather than assumed. See the next section on putting a number on time.

4. A fixed monthly contribution into a shared pot

Each sibling puts an agreed amount into a joint account or a parent's account each month, and shared costs are paid from it. This avoids constant settling up, but someone still has to track what was spent and top it up when big costs come in.

To settle up, C sends $300 to A and $200 to B. If the family had agreed to count A's hours driving to appointments as a contribution, A's balance would be higher still.

Counting time, not just money

The sibling who lives closest often ends up doing the driving, the appointments, the calls with the care agency and the late-night emergencies. If only money counts, that work is invisible, and resentment builds.

Some ways families handle it:

  • Log the hours without converting them to money. Just making the work visible changes the conversation.
  • Agree an hourly value for care time, for example what you'd otherwise pay for a home care worker, and count it toward that sibling's share.
  • Cover the hands-on sibling's costs fully (gas, parking, meals out on hospital days) as shared expenses, even if time isn't counted.
  • Pay a sibling directly for care they provide. If you go this route, get advice first: payment for care can have tax consequences for the sibling receiving it.

Tax rules that affect who pays

Who pays for something can decide who can claim it. A few CRA rules worth knowing before you settle on a split:

  • Medical expenses for a parent who depends on you go on line 33199. Keep receipts and proof of who paid. If one sibling pays the pharmacy and another reimburses them, agree whose claim it is so the same receipt isn't claimed twice. More in claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent.
  • The Canada caregiver credit can be split between siblings who support the same parent, but the combined claims can't go over the maximum (CRA's line 30450 page). See how to claim the Canada caregiver credit.
  • Credits only help someone who owes tax. Both are non-refundable, so they're usually worth more to a sibling with taxable income. Some families route eligible expenses through that sibling's card and adjust the cash split to match. Talk to a tax professional before you do this; the claimant needs to meet CRA's conditions, including that your parent depended on them for support.

Write it down and settle up regularly

A short written agreement, even an email everyone replies "yes" to, prevents most arguments. Include:

  1. What counts as a shared cost, and what doesn't
  2. The split (equal, by percentage, or custom), and whether time counts
  3. How spending gets recorded: one shared spreadsheet or app, with receipts
  4. How often you settle up (monthly works for most families)
  5. Who claims which tax credits, and how the caregiver credit is divided
  6. When you'll revisit it: after a move into care, a big change in someone's income, or once a year

The single biggest help is a shared record everyone can see. When each sibling can check what's been spent and by whom, there's much less to argue about.
