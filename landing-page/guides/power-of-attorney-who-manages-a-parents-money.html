Power of attorney: who manages a parent's money

A power of attorney is how a parent chooses, ahead of time, who can handle their money or health decisions if they can't. Without one, families can end up in court during a crisis. This guide covers how it works in Ontario, and how siblings can share the load.

Two kinds of power of attorney

This section describes Ontario, based on the Law Commission of Ontario's report on powers of attorney. Other provinces use different terms and rules, and Quebec uses different tools altogether, so check yours.

  • For property covers money and financial decisions: paying bills, managing accounts, selling a home. A continuing power of attorney for property can take effect right away and continue if your parent loses capacity, or it can be set to take effect only once incapacity is found. Your parent chooses which.
  • For personal care covers decisions such as health care, shelter and daily needs. It takes effect only once your parent loses legal capacity.

Making one

Your parent must have the legal capacity to give one, which is why the conversation is best had early. In Ontario it needs two independent witnesses over 18. The witnesses can't be the attorney or the attorney's spouse or partner, the person giving the power or their spouse or partner, or their child. A continuing power of attorney for property needs a statement that it's meant to continue through incapacity. Legal advice isn't required by law in Ontario, though a lawyer can help your parent think through who to name and what to say. The government's standard form is optional.

What the attorney has to do

  • An attorney for property has a fiduciary duty: a high standard of loyalty and care toward your parent.
  • An attorney for personal care must act diligently and in good faith.
  • All attorneys must keep accounts of what they do.
  • They must explain their role to your parent, encourage supportive contact with family and friends, and consult them from time to time.

Accounts aren't routinely filed. A court can order them to be passed on application, and can suspend or end the power of attorney after reviewing them. In practice, that means openness within the family is what protects everyone.

If there isn't one

For personal-care decisions, Ontario's Health Care Consent Act provides a ranked list of substitute decision-makers, usually starting with a spouse or partner and then other family members. For property, a guardianship application is generally needed, and the Public Guardian and Trustee or a court may get involved. Guardianship is more restrictive than a power of attorney.

Siblings and the power of attorney

  • Talk about who, before it's urgent. Often the sibling who lives nearest is named; make sure the others know and understand why.
  • Treat the role as a job, not a verdict. Being named doesn't mean that sibling pays more, and not being named doesn't mean you're not trusted. Say so out loud.
  • Keep your parent's money separate from anyone's own, with a record and receipts for every payment. Share that record with your siblings regularly.
  • Use your parent's money first. The attorney's job is to use your parent's resources for your parent's needs; what's left over is what siblings may need to share. See how to split a parent's care costs fairly.
  • Know the costs. Knowing what care costs helps the attorney plan; see too when a sibling isn't helping if contributions are uneven.

A short checklist

  1. Ask your parent, while they can decide, whether they have a power of attorney, and where it's kept.
  2. If not, suggest they speak to a lawyer in your province.
  3. Tell all siblings who was named for property and for personal care.
  4. Agree how the attorney will share accounts with the family.
  5. Review it after a major change such as a move, an illness or a death in the family.
