Care costs

What home care, retirement homes and long-term care cost in Canada

The cost of care depends on the kind of care, the province and how much public funding your parent qualifies for. Here is how the three main options work in Canada, the long-term care fees set by each province, and how to get accurate numbers for your own family.

Last reviewed November 17, 2026. Prices change often; check the linked sources.

Three kinds of care, three ways of paying

  • Long-term care (nursing home). For people with high care needs. Publicly funded; the resident pays a regulated fee and the province covers the rest. Admission usually goes through a provincial assessment, and waits can be long.
  • Retirement homes and residences. Private housing with meals and optional care. The resident pays, and the home sets the price.
  • Care at home. Public home care programs exist in each province, but the hours are limited. Families often add privately paid personal support workers (PSWs) on top.

Long-term care: set by the province

Long-term care fees are set by each government and usually change on a fixed date every year. In Ontario, for example, the maximum basic accommodation charge rose to $70.00 a day ($2,129.17 a month) on July 1, 2026, from $68.56 a day ($2,085.37 a month), according to a notice from a long-term care home. Semi-private and private rooms cost more, and Ontario has a rate reduction program for residents in basic accommodation with low income.

Retirement homes: set by the home

There's no government price list for retirement homes, and Canada's national seniors' rental housing survey from CMHC has stopped collecting this data, so we don't quote an average here. Prices vary by city, building, suite size and care level. A few habits help:

  • Ask each home for its full written fee schedule, not just the base rent.
  • Find out what's included (meals, housekeeping, activities, an emergency response system) and what's extra (care packages, medication management, parking, extra meals).
  • Ask how care fees change as needs increase, and how much notice you get before increases.
  • Ontario licenses retirement homes and regulates fee increases and care services; other provinces regulate them to different degrees.

Home care and PSW hours

Publicly funded home care exists in every province, but the hours and eligibility vary. Call your province's home care program for an assessment; it's free to ask, and what they approve sets your baseline before you add any private hours.

Private rates vary by agency and city. As one example, a Thunder Bay agency lists roughly $35 to $45 an hour for personal support and companionship, and $40 to $50 an hour for specialized support such as dementia or post-hospital care. It doesn't date those rates, so treat them as a rough guide. Its own illustration, at $40 an hour, comes to about $520 a month for three hours a week and about $6,933 a month for 40 hours a week (Senior Care Access). Independent PSWs can cost less, but then you're the employer, so check what that involves.

Which costs may help at tax time

Some of these costs can be claimed as medical expenses, and the Canada caregiver credit may also apply. What's claimable depends on the kind of care and whether your parent qualifies for the disability tax credit; our guide to claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent goes through it, and the caregiver credit guide covers the other credit. Keep itemized receipts from the home or agency.

How to get real numbers for your family

  1. Ask for a needs assessment from your province's home and community care program.
  2. List your parent's income and savings first. That sets what they can contribute before anyone else is asked; see splitting a parent's care costs fairly between siblings.
  3. Get written fee schedules from two or three homes or agencies.
  4. Put the options side by side in one shared place so every sibling sees the same figures.
  5. Recheck each year; fees rise and your parent's needs change.
