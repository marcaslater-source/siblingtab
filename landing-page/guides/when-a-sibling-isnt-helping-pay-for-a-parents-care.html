Family money

When a sibling isn't helping pay for a parent's care

When one sibling does the driving and the paying and another contributes little, the money is rarely the whole story. Here is a calm, practical way to raise it, what to offer besides cash, and what to do if talking doesn't work.

Why contributions end up uneven

It's tempting to read a quiet sibling as not caring. Often it's something more ordinary:

  • They can't see the costs. If receipts live in one person's wallet, the others genuinely may not know what Mom or Dad's care costs each month.
  • Money and capacity differ. One sibling may be stretched by a mortgage, kids or a job loss. Another may live far away and feel unable to help any way except money, or the reverse.
  • Nobody agreed on a split. Without a conversation, each sibling quietly assumes a different one.
  • Different views of what's needed. One person sees a PSW as essential and another sees it as optional.

Starting from "I assume they don't know" rather than "they don't care" keeps the conversation open, and it's often true.

Start with the facts, not the feelings

Before you raise it, put the numbers on one page: what's been spent in the last three to six months, who paid for what, and what's coming (a move into care, a new prescription, home care hours). Share the same page with everyone. A neutral record is much harder to argue with than someone's memory, and it removes the "you never told me" problem. Make a specific, easy ask

"I need more help" is hard to say yes to. A specific request is easy to answer. Compare:

  • Vague: "You never pitch in."
  • Specific: "Dad's care is about $X a month beyond his pension. I'd like the three of us to cover it by percentage. Could you take $Y a month, or would you rather take over the pharmacy and appointment bookings?"

Speak about your own situation ("I'm finding it hard to carry this on my own") rather than their behaviour. Offer a couple of options and a date to reply by, and put it in writing so there is a record that the question was asked.

Ways to contribute besides equal cash

Equal shares don't suit every family. Some alternatives, all of which are fair if everyone agrees to them:

  • By income. Each sibling pays a percentage that reflects what they can afford.
  • Money for time. One sibling handles appointments and visits; another covers more of the bills.
  • Taking a specific job. Managing the paperwork, the insurance claims, the tax receipts, the weekly call with the care agency.
  • A small, fixed monthly amount from someone with limited means, so everyone is contributing something and nobody feels like a spectator.

It also helps to write down the hours people give, even if you never turn them into dollars. Seeing who is covering what often shifts the conversation by itself.

Bringing in a neutral person

If two or three attempts haven't moved anything, a third party can lower the temperature:

  • A family meeting led by someone everyone respects, such as an aunt, a family friend or your parent's doctor or care coordinator.
  • A professional mediator. Many mediators work with families on elder-care disputes and can help in a single session.
  • Your parent's lawyer or financial advisor, who can explain what your parent's own money can cover before anyone is asked to contribute.

Involve your parent where they're able to take part. Their own wishes about their care, and what they'd want paid for, often settle questions siblings can't.

What the law says

Most families never need this, and it is a last resort that tends to damage relationships, but it helps to know it exists. In Ontario, section 32 of the Family Law Act says that every adult child has an obligation to provide support, in accordance with need, for a parent who has cared for or supported them, to the extent the child is capable of doing so. Rules differ from province to province and from case to case. If you are considering a legal route, talk to a lawyer in your province first.

Protecting yourself while it plays out

  • Keep records. Save every receipt and note who paid. They also support your tax claims; see claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent.
  • Keep your parent's money separate. If you manage their accounts, don't mix them with yours.
  • Claim what you're entitled to. The Canada caregiver credit and medical expense claims can offset some of what you've paid.
  • Set your own limit. Decide what you can afford without putting your own retirement or health at risk, and say so.
  • Revisit it. Circumstances change. A sibling who can't help now may be able to in a year, and an agreement that's reviewed regularly is easier to keep.
