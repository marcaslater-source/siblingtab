Family caregiving

When one sibling does most of the caregiving

In many families one person ends up doing most of the driving, the appointments, the phone calls and the worrying, often without anyone deciding that. Here is how to make that work visible, ask for help in a way people can say yes to, and look after yourself while you sort it out.

Recent conversation online, including a widely shared sketch-comedy bit about unequal caregiving between siblings, has a lot of people saying "that's my family." If you are the sibling carrying most of the load, or the one who suspects a brother or sister is, you are far from alone. This guide is about what to do next, calmly.

It usually happens by default

Very few families sit down and agree that one child will do most of the caregiving. It tends to happen quietly:

  • One sibling lives closest, so they are the one the hospital or the retirement home calls.
  • One sibling has a more flexible job, or no children at home, so they are assumed to have the time.
  • One sibling is simply the organized one, or the one a parent has always leaned on.
  • A first small favour becomes a weekly routine, then a role, and nobody ever renegotiates it.

Seeing it as a pattern that grew, rather than as a verdict on anyone's character, makes the next steps easier for everyone.

Make the work visible

Siblings who live elsewhere often genuinely cannot see how much goes into a week of care. Before you raise it, put it on one page. For two or three weeks, jot down:

  • Time: appointments, visits, phone calls with doctors or the care agency, errands, paperwork, and the unplanned emergencies.
  • Money: what you paid out of pocket (gas, parking, pharmacy, groceries, supplies) with receipts.
  • Mental load: the things you track that nobody else sees, such as renewals, refills, the next appointment, who is checking in on Mom or Dad.

You do not have to turn hours into dollars. Simply seeing "11 hours and $180 this week" lands differently from "I do everything."

Start the conversation

People respond better to a specific, shared problem than to a complaint about their behaviour. A few things that help:

  • Share the page, not a verdict. "Here is what a typical week looks like for Dad. I would like us to work out how to share it."
  • Talk about your own situation. "I am running low and I need this to change" is hard to argue with in a way that "you never help" is not.
  • Assume they do not know. Often it is true, and it keeps the door open.
  • Make a specific ask with options and a date. "Could you take over the pharmacy and the insurance paperwork, or put in $X a month? Let me know by the end of the month."
  • Choose the setting. A planned call or meeting works better than a text thread, or a conversation in the hospital corridor. See how to hold a family meeting about a parent's care.

Keep your parent in the picture where they are able. What they want, and what they would like each child to do, often settles things siblings cannot.

Ways to share the load

Fair does not have to mean identical. Common arrangements that families find workable if everyone agrees to them:

  • Split by task. One person owns appointments, another owns the money and receipts, another owns the weekly visit or call, another handles the home and groceries.
  • Trade money for time. The sibling who does the hands-on work contributes less cash, and siblings with more income cover more of the bills.
  • Count the hands-on sibling's costs as shared. Gas, parking and meals out on hospital days should not come out of one person's pocket.
  • Schedule relief. A regular weekend or week when someone else takes over, so the main caregiver can rest and does not have to ask each time.
  • Pay for help. Where the budget allows, a PSW or an adult day program can take hours off one person's week, and the cost can be shared. See what home care and long-term care cost in Canada.
  • Pay a sibling for care. Some families do this. Get tax and legal advice first, because payment for care can have tax consequences for the person receiving it.

Write the arrangement down, even as a short email everyone replies "yes" to, and agree a date to revisit it. Needs change, and an agreement that gets reviewed is far easier to keep. More on dividing costs is in how to split a parent's care costs fairly.

If you live far away

If you are the sibling at a distance, you may be feeling guilty, or defensive. There is a lot you can take on without being in the room:

  • Phone calls with the doctor's office, the pharmacy, the insurer or the care agency.
  • Paperwork and tax: gathering receipts, applying for credits, filing claims.
  • Managing the shared record of spending, so the local sibling does not have to.
  • Booking and paying for respite or a PSW.
  • Planned visits that give the local sibling a real break, not just a visit you both have to host.

The most useful first step is to ask, "What is the one thing that would help most this month?" and then do it reliably.

Protecting your own work, money and health

  • Set a limit. Decide what you can give without putting your job, your retirement savings or your health at risk, and say it out loud. Caregivers who push past their limit often end up unable to help at all.
  • Keep every receipt. They support your tax claims and your case in any later discussion about who paid what. See claiming medical expenses you paid for a parent and the Canada caregiver credit.
  • Keep your parent's money separate from yours. If you are managing their accounts, see power of attorney: who manages a parent's money.
  • Look after yourself. Your own doctor's appointments, sleep and friendships are not optional extras. Many communities have caregiver support groups, and your parent's care team can often point you to one.

Support and leave in Canada

Government programs exist for people who step back from work to care for a family member. The federal Employment Insurance program has caregiving benefits for some situations, and provinces and territories have their own job-protected leaves, so check the details for your province and your situation. Your parent's doctor, hospital discharge planner or your province's home and community care program can also tell you what publicly funded help is available in your area. The official Government of Canada page below is a good place to start.

Keeping it fair over time

The families who keep this from turning into a long-term grievance tend to do three things: they keep one shared record that everyone can see, they say out loud who is covering what, and they check in regularly so nobody has to ask for help twice. A shared record of spending, and the hours people give, turns "I feel like I do everything" into something the whole family can look at together.
